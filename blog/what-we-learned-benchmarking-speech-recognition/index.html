Dashie Blog / What we learned benchmarking speech recognition
Benchmark · Voice & AI

We benchmarked six speech recognition engines. The results changed our minds.

John·Dashie Developer·August 2026·5 min read

Dashie can send your voice to the cloud, or keep it entirely on the tablet. We wanted to know what that choice actually costs you — in accuracy, in speed, and in memory. So we measured it properly.

Why the usual numbers don't help

Published speech-recognition benchmarks are almost all run on read speech — audiobook passages, seven or eight seconds long, in literary prose. That is nothing like talking to a dashboard on your kitchen counter. Real commands are short and plain: “turn off the kitchen lights”, “what's on the calendar today”, “set a timer for ten minutes”.

So we built a corpus in that shape: 641 clips between 1.3 and 3 seconds, from 75 different speakers. Then we ran every engine twice — once clean, and once with a real room's acoustics and kitchen noise mixed in at a realistic level, because that is the condition a family kitchen actually presents.

Lower is better — these are word error rates, so 4% means roughly one word in twenty-five comes back wrong.

Four things surprised us

1. Self-hosting beats the cloud — if you have a real GPU. We had previously concluded it didn't. That conclusion turned out to be a statement about the laptop we tested on, not about self-hosting. On actual GPU hardware, Whisper turbo is both more accurate than the cloud service and fast enough to feel identical. If you already run a machine with a graphics card, that is a genuinely better option than paying anyone.

2. Noise is where the cheap models fall apart. Look at the two accuracy columns. The cloud and GPU engines degrade gently — four percent becomes five. The smallest on-device model doubles, from 7.3% to 15.9%. A quiet-room benchmark would have told you these engines were closer than they are. Kitchens are not quiet rooms.

3. On a Raspberry Pi, the wait is the problem. Every other engine in that table answers in roughly seven to eight tenths of a second — near enough identical that you would not be able to tell them apart by feel. The Pi takes about 2.2 seconds. That is the difference between a dashboard that replies and one where you start wondering whether it heard you, and it is on top of the Pi also being among the least accurate options. If you are running Home Assistant's built-in speech on a Pi, that lag is not your imagination and it is not your network.

4. Memory is a real constraint, and it is not free. The better on-device model needs somewhere between 140 and 206 MB while it works — a wide range, and we quote it as one because it genuinely varies that much run to run. On a 2 GB tablet that is real headroom to ask for, and it is the reason the smallest model shipped first even though it is the least accurate.

That number used to be far worse. The model we ship is packaged in a format that had quietly stored every weight in the file twice — about 52 MB of pure duplication. Repackaging it cut the download from 111 MB to 48 MB and took roughly 135 MB off what it needs to run. It costs about a third of a second more per response, which on a memory-constrained tablet we think is a trade worth making.

How we tried not to fool ourselves. Every engine was fed identical audio, scored with the same open normalizer the published leaderboards use, and measured from the moment the speaker stops talking — not from when the file ends, which quietly flatters streaming engines. Several early results turned out to be our own measurement mistakes rather than real findings: a memory reading that was actually the tablet's photo widget, a latency figure that was the wrong stopwatch. We threw those out and re-ran them.

What this means for Dashie

There is no single winner, and we have stopped looking for one. What there is instead is a real trade — accuracy against privacy against the memory budget of the device on your counter — and different families will weigh it differently.

What the numbers did change is which on-device model we are willing to ship. The smallest one is measurably the weakest engine we tested, and under noise it is no better than a Raspberry Pi. That is not the experience we want to be the default.

If you run Home Assistant with a GPU box already, point Dashie at it. If you want everything to stay on the tablet, that works and it is honest about what it costs. And if you want the best accuracy for the least setup, the cloud option is still excellent and cheap.

* About the SpeechRecognizer number. Android's built-in recogniser is the one engine that cannot be handed a recording — it insists on listening to a live microphone. So while every other engine ate the exact same audio file, this one had to listen to clips played aloud in a room, which is a harder test than the others faced. It also declines to answer at all on roughly half of these clips rather than return a low-confidence guess, so we scored it only on the ones it answered, and re-scored the other engines on that same subset for a fair comparison. Both of those effects push in opposite directions — one flatters it, one penalises it — which is why we are comfortable saying “roughly in this range” and not comfortable publishing a precise rate. We have not measured its response time or its accuracy in noise at all. What we can say confidently is the memory: it borrows about 75 MB from a system service that is already running, and gives it back within seconds — where the on-device Whisper-style models hold their memory for as long as the app is open.

Methodology notes: corpus is LibriSpeech test-clean and dev-clean, filtered to 1.3–3 second utterances; noisy condition adds domestic room impulse responses and recorded kitchen noise at 10 dB SNR. Word error rates use Whisper's English normalizer so they are comparable to published figures. Response time is measured from the moment the speaker stops talking to a finished transcript, and includes the half-second of silence every engine waits through to decide you are done. Memory is the engine's own cost on a Samsung SM-X200 — the increase in proportional set size from before loading to peak, on a freshly started app, averaged over three runs. Where we give a range it is because the figure genuinely moved that much between runs, and a single number would imply a precision we did not measure. GPU figures were measured on a rented A100 — a fast card, so treat the speed as a ceiling rather than what a home machine delivers.
